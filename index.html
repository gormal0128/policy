한 컨설턴트는 이번 경매를 “놓친 기회”라고 표현했으며, 다른 한 명은 단순히 규제 기관의 우선순위를 반영한 것이라고 말했습니다.



최소 가격을 초과하는 수익은 추가적인 커버리지 의무로 전환될 예정이었습니다.



총 5개의 블록이 708-718 MHz 및 763-773 MHz 대역에서 각각 2 x 10 MHz로 경매에 부쳐졌습니다. 아마존이아(Amazonia), 브리사넷(Brisanet), 유니피케(Unifique), 이에즈! 텔레콤(Iez! Telecom)이 모든 가용 스펙트럼을 확보했습니다. 각 지역 운영자는 하나의 블록을 획득했으며, 브리사넷은 두 개의 블록을 획득했습니다. 이 5개의 블록은 첫 번째 라운드에서 최소 가격으로 판매되었습니다. 라이센스는 2044년 12월까지 유효합니다.



이번 경매에서 아나텔(Anatel)은 현재 4G 커버리지가 없는 지역, 농촌 지역, 그리고 커버리지가 없는 특정 연방 고속도로 구간을 선정했다고 컬렌 인터내셔널(Cullen International)의 분석가인 페드로 미란다(Pedro Miranda)가 말했습니다.

이번 경매는 2021년에 열린 경매에서 3.5 GHz 대역의 스펙트럼을 획득한 지역 운영자들에게 유리하게 설계되었습니다. 아마존이아는 그 경매에서 성공적인 입찰자가 아니었지만, 지역 통신사인 세르콤텔(Sercomtel)로부터 3.5 GHz 라이센스를 확보했기 때문에 이번 경매에 참여할 수 있었습니다.



커버리지 의무: “놓친 기회”?

NERA 경제 컨설팅의 매니징 디렉터인 리차드 마스든(Richard Marsden)은 이번 경매를 위해 한 모바일 운영자와 밀접하게 작업했다고 밝혔습니다. 그는 흥미로운 요소로, 최소 가격을 초과하는 각 레알(현지 통화)이 추가적인 커버리지 의무로 전환될 예정이었다고 언급했습니다.

그러나 모든 블록이 최소 가격으로 판매되었기 때문에 추가적인 커버리지 의무는 발생하지 않았습니다.

또한, 스펙트럼이 첫 단계에서 판매되었기 때문에, 어떤 국가 모바일 운영자도 입찰 과정에 참여할 수 없었습니다.

미란다는 기존의 전국 운영자들이 “입찰할 기회를 갖고 싶어 했다”는 점에서 “좌절감”을 느끼고 있다고 말했습니다. 이는 더 높은 입찰가와 스펙트럼에 대한 더 높은 가격을 의미했을 것입니다.

“그들이 [지역 운영자들] 최소 가격을 지불했기 때문에 추가 의무를 위한 추가 자금이 없어서, 의무 이상의 일은 일어나지 않을 것입니다.”라고 마스든은 말하며 이번 경매를 “놓친 기회”로 평가했습니다.

“소규모 플레이어를 위해 스펙트럼을 예약하기로 한 결정은 스펙트럼이 최소 가격에 판매되었음을 의미합니다.”라고 그는 덧붙였습니다. “다르게 진행되었다면, 아마 가격이 훨씬 더 높았을 것이고, 브라질 정부는 브라질 내 네트워크 구축을 위한 훨씬 더 많은 의무를 확보했을 것입니다.”



결과는 규제 기관이 예상한 것과 정확히 일치합니다.



아나텔의 목표를 반영한 것?

그러나 이번 경매는 단순히 아나텔의 목표를 반영한 것이라고 미란다는 말했습니다. “놓친 기회”라고 부르는 것은 “경매를 어떻게 프레임하느냐”와 “경매에서 원하는 결과”에 따라 달라진다고 그는 설명했습니다.

“아나텔의 관점에서 볼 때, 지역 운영자들을 강화하여 브라질의 각 지역에 추가적인 네 번째 운영자를 도입하려는 노력의 일환으로, 결과는 그들이 예상한 바와 정확히 일치합니다.”라고 미란다는 말했습니다. “블록당 입찰자가 하나뿐이라면, 최소 가격만 받을 것이라는 것은 명백합니다.”

2026년 12월부터 2030년 12월까지 운영자들은 매년 20%의 위치를 커버해야 합니다. 운영자들은 배치 일정에 맞추기 위한 계획을 아나텔에 제출해야 하며, 아나텔은 그들의 네트워크 배치를 감독할 것입니다.

“아나텔은 소비자에게 더 많은 선택을 제공하려고 하며, 서비스 품질과 가격이 개선되기를 희망하고 있습니다.”라고 미란다는 덧붙였습니다.



앞으로의 전망

700 MHz 대역에서 FDD에서 TDD로의 전환 가능성에 대해 미란다는 현재 라이센스를 보유한 운영자와 새로운 지역 라이센스 보유자들이 그들의 네트워크의 대부분을 위해 스펙트럼에 의존하고 있기 때문에 라이센스가 종료되기 전에 대역 사용 방식을 변경하는 것은 아마도 너무 복잡할 것이라고 말했습니다.

배치 측면에서 미란다는 브리사넷과 유니피케가 이미 시작을 했고 “정말 속도를 내고 있다”고 언급했으며, 다른 운영자들은 “올해의 배치 마감일을 맞추기 위해 속도를 내야 할 것”이라고 덧붙였습니다.

그러나 에두아르도 투데(Eduardo Tude), 텔레코(Teleco) 컨설팅 회사의 창립자이자 CEO는 지역 운영자들이 네트워크를 배치하는 데 어려움이 없을 것이라고 말했습니다.

마스든은 브라질의 경매 형식이 효율적인 스펙트럼 할당을 보장하지 않는다고 제안했습니다.

“앞으로 나아가면서, 브라질이 다수의 라운드로 진행되는 상승식 입찰 경매로 전환하는 것이 더 나을지에 대한 흥미로운 질문이 있다고 생각합니다. 이는 현재 대부분의 주요 경제국들이 시행하고 있는 방식입니다.”라고 그는 말했습니다.

미란다는 “앞으로 몇 년 동안 이 지역 운영자들이 전국 운영자들과 시장 점유율을 두고 경쟁하는 모습을 지켜보는 것이 흥미로울 것”이라고 말했습니다.

“그 경쟁이 실제로 어떤 결과를 가져올지는 시간이 지나야 알 수 있습니다.”라고 마스든은 덧붙였습니다. “분명히, 일반적으로 전 세계 대부분의 시장은 이제 세 개의 플레이어가 있으며, 네 번째 플레이어의 성과는 매우 다양하지만, 종종 지속 가능하지 않습니다.”

One consultant described the auction as a “missed opportunity,” while another said it simply reflected the regulator’s priorities.





Any revenue above the reserve price would have been converted into additional coverage obligations



In total, five blocks of 2 x 10 MHz in the 708-718 MHz and 763-773 MHz bands were auctioned. Amazonia, Brisanet, Unifique and Iez! Telecom secured all the available spectrum. Each regional operator won one block, with the exception of Brisanet, which won two. The five blocks were sold at the reserve price in the first round. The licences will be valid until December 2044.



For this award, Anatel selected areas that currently do not have 4G coverage, rural areas, and sections of selected federal highways with no coverage, said Pedro Miranda, analyst at Cullen International. 

The auction was designed to favour regional operators who won spectrum in the 3.5 GHz band at an auction held in 2021. Amazonia was not a successful bidder in that auction, but could take part in this one as it had obtained 3.5 GHz licences from regional telco Sercomtel.

Coverage obligations: a “missed opportunity”?

Richard Marsden, managing director at NERA Economic Consulting, was closely involved in the work of one of the mobile operators for the auction. He said that an interesting element was that each real (local currency) that went above the reserve price was to be converted into additional coverage obligations. 

However, as all the blocks were sold for the reserve price, no additional coverage obligations were triggered.

In addition, because the spectrum was sold in the first stage, no national mobile operator could take part in the bidding process. 

Miranda said that the “frustration” from incumbent nationwide operators is that they wanted to “have the chance to bid,” which would have meant higher bids and higher prices for the spectrum.

“Because they [the regional operators] paid the reserve price, there’s no additional money for additional obligations, so nothing beyond the mandatory will happen,” said Marsden, who considered the auction a “missed opportunity”.

“The decision to reserve the spectrum for the small players means that the spectrum sold at reserve,” he said. “Had it been done differently, maybe the price would have been much higher, and the Brazilian government would have secured a lot more obligations to build out the network in Brazil.”



The outcome is exactly what the regulator expected



A reflection of Anatel’s aims?

However, the auction was simply a reflection of Anatel’s aims, said Miranda. Calling it a “missed opportunity” depends on “what you frame the auction as” and “the outcome you want from the auction,” he said. 

“From Anatel’s perspective, whereby it is trying to strengthen regional operators to promote competition with an additional fourth operator in each of Brazil’s regions, the outcome is exactly what they expected,” Miranda said. “If you only have one bidder per block, it’s obvious that you’re only going to get the reserve price.”

From December 2026 until December 2030, operators will have to cover 20% of the locations each year. The operators will need to present a plan to Anatel for meeting the deployment schedule. Anatel will then oversee the deployment of their networks.

 “Anatel is trying to offer more choices to consumers in the hope that quality of service and prices will improve,” said Miranda. 

Looking ahead

On a potential switch from FDD to TDD in the 700 MHz band, Miranda said current licensees and new regional licensees in the 700 MHz band depend on the spectrum for much of their network. This means it would probably be too complex to change how the band is used before the licences end.

In terms of deployment, Miranda said that Brisanet and Unifique had made a start and had already “really picked up speed,” while others will “need to speed up to meet this year’s deployment deadlines”. 

However, Eduardo Tude, founder and CEO of Teleco, a consulting company and Brazilian telecoms information portal, said the regional operators would have no difficulty deploying the networks.  

Marsden suggested that the Brazilian auction format did not guarantee efficient spectrum allocation. 

“Looking forward, I think an interesting question is whether Brazil would be better off moving to running multi-round, ascending bid auctions with simultaneous switching across lots, which most leading economies around the world do now,” he said.

It will be “interesting to watch the next few years to see how well these regional operators do in trying to compete with nationwide operators for market share,” Miranda said. 

“Whether that competition will deliver, in practice, time will tell,” said Marsden. “Obviously, in general, most markets worldwide are now three players, and the track records of fourth players vary a lot, but often they’re not sustainable.”•

유럽의 산업 단체들이 라디오 스펙트럼 정책 그룹(RSPG)의 6G 스펙트럼 로드맵에 대한 의견을 제시하고 있습니다. RSPG는 이와 관련된 “의견”을 6월에 최종 확정할 것으로 기대하고 있습니다.



상위 6GHz 대역은 “이 세기 말에 6G를 출시하기 위한 유럽의 유일한 실현 가능한 스펙트럼 옵션”입니다.



2026년 2월에 발표될 RSPG 6G 로드맵은 유럽연합(EU)에 대한 6G에 필요한 스펙트럼 대역에 대한 조언의 초안입니다. 이에 대한 공청회는 3월에 마감되었습니다. RSPG는 6월 16일에 전체 회의를 개최할 예정이며, 이 자리에서 로드맵의 최종 버전을 승인할 수 있기를 희망하고 있습니다. 6G는 2030년경에 출시될 수 있기를 기대하고 있습니다.



6GHz 대역

RSPG의 의견은 상위 6GHz(6425-7125MHz) 대역이 “2030년까지 유럽에서 6G 도입에 적합한 주요 대역”이라고 제안하고 있습니다. 모바일 산업 단체인 GSMA와 Connect Europe는 이 대역이 “이 세기 말에 6G를 출시하기 위한 유일한 실현 가능한 스펙트럼 옵션”이라고 생각하며, 특히 “효율적인 6G 롤아웃을 위해서는 운영자당 최소 200MHz가 필요하다”고 지적하고 있습니다.



“추가적인 중간 대역 스펙트럼이 없다면, 전 세계 도시 인구의 50% 이상을 차지하는 도시들이 2030년까지 용량 제약을 받을 수 있다”고 GSMA의 최근 보고서는 밝혔습니다. 이러한 이유로 RSPG의 초안 의견은 “운영자들이 투자 계획을 세우고, 생태계 개발을 지원하며, 국제 조화 논의에 참여할 수 있도록 허용하기 때문에 긍정적”이라고 두 기관은 말했습니다.



장비 공급업체들도 RSPG의 견해를 환영하고 있습니다. 예를 들어, 노키아는 이 대역을 유럽의 6G를 위한 “주요 초석”으로 설명하며, 여러 운영자와 함께 현장 시험을 수행했으며, 이를 통해 상위 6GHz 대역이 기존의 매크로 사이트를 재사용하면서 3.5GHz와 유사한 다중 기가비트 성능을 제공할 수 있음을 주장하고 있습니다. 장비 공급업체들은 이 대역에 대한 명확한 로드맵이 조기 6G 연구 개발을 정당화하고, 공통의 유럽 장치 및 네트워크 생태계를 만드는 데 필수적이라고 노키아는 언급했습니다.



에릭슨은 또한 6G가 센티미터 대역인 7-15GHz에서 상당한 새로운 스펙트럼이 필요할 것이라고 주장하며, 특히 오늘날의 중간 대역 보유와 가까운 대역에 중점을 두고 있습니다. 최근 6G 백서에서는 7.125-8.4GHz 대역이 광역 용량과 이동성에 특히 중요하다고 강조하고 있으며, 최신 이동성 보고서와 퀄컴과의 6G 시연에서는 6-8GHz 프로토타입 캐리어 및 장치 기능을 탐구하고 있습니다.



며칠 전, 중국 산업정보화부(MIIT)는 자국이 6G를 위해 6GHz 대역을 사용할 계획이라고 발표했으며, 이는 6GHz 대역을 글로벌 차원에서 6G에 사용하는 주장을 강화할 것입니다.



UHF 대역의 6G

초안 의견은 RSPG와 CEPT가 “470-694MHz 대역의 유연한 사용을 위한 다양한 시나리오를 평가하고” “IMT 또는 PPDR와 같은 모바일 서비스의 대체 용도로 700MHz 이하의 대역을 재목적화하려는 행정기관을 위한 전략적 공통 지침을 제공할 것”이라고 명시하고 있습니다.



PMSE(프로그램 제작 및 특별 이벤트) 및 방송 부문에서는, 이는 2030년 이후 유연한 사용 시나리오를 통해 PMSE가 여전히 보호된 접근을 누릴 수 있는 몇 안 되는 UHF 블록 중 하나의 점진적인 침식으로 이어질 수 있다는 우려가 있습니다.

Industry bodies in Europe have been having their say on the Radio Spectrum Policy Group’s 6G spectrum roadmap. The RSPG expects to finalise its “Opinion” on this in June.





The upper 6 GHz band is the ‘only feasible spectrum option in Europe to launch 6G at the end of this decade’



Published in February 2026, the RSPG 6G roadmap is a draft version of its advice for the European Union on which spectrum bands will be needed for 6G in Europe. The consultation on it closed in March.

The RSPG will gather for a plenary meeting on 16 June, when it hopes to be able to approve a final version of the roadmap. The hope is that 6G can be launched around 2030.

6 GHz band

The RSPG Opinion suggests that the upper 6 GHz (6425-7125 MHz) band is the “primary band suitable for the introduction of 6G in Europe by 2030”.

Mobile industry bodies the GSMA and Connect Europe both support this as they think the band is the “only feasible spectrum option in Europe to launch 6G at the end of this decade,” particularly as a minimum of 200 MHz per operator is needed “for efficient 6G rollouts”.  

“Without additional mid-band spectrum, cities covering more than 50% of the global urban population could become capacity constrained by 2030,” a recent GSMA report said. For this reason, the RSPG draft Opinion is positive as it allows “operators to plan investments, support ecosystem development, and engage in international harmonisation discussions,” the two organisations said. 

Equipment vendors have also welcomed the RSPG’s view. Nokia, for example, has described the band as a potential “cornerstone for 6G” in Europe and has already carried out field trials with several operators, which it claims show that the upper 6 GHz band can reuse existing macro sites while delivering multi‑gigabit performance comparable to 3.5 GHz.

Equipment suppliers see a clear roadmap for the band as vital to justify early 6G R&D and to create a common European device and network ecosystem, Nokia said.

Ericsson has also argued that 6G will need substantial new spectrum in the centimetric 7–15 GHz range, with a particular focus on bands close to today’s mid‑band holdings. In a recent 6G white paper, it singles out 7.125–8.4 GHz as especially important for wide‑area capacity and mobility, while its latest Mobility Report and 6G demonstrations with Qualcomm explore 6–8 GHz prototype carriers and device capabilities.

A few days ago, China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT) announced that the country intends to use the 6 GHz band for 6G, a decision that will undoubtedly strengthen the argument for using the 6 GHz band for 6G on a global scale.

6G in UHF bands

The draft Opinion says that the RSPG and CEPT will “assess different scenarios for flexible use of the 470–694 MHz band” and “provide strategic common guidance on a potential way forward for those administrations wishing to repurpose spectrum within the sub‑700 MHz band for alternative uses in the mobile service, such as IMT or PPDR”.

For the PMSE and broadcasting sector, the concern is that this could open the door, via flexible‑use scenarios after 2030, to a gradual erosion of one of the few remaining UHF blocks where PMSE (programme making and special events) still enjoys protected access.

In particular, paragraph 10 of the Opinion states: “The RSPG notes that the 470-694 MHz band has already been allocated to the mobile, except aeronautical mobile, service on a secondary basis in almost all EU countries and could enhance nationwide and indoor 6G coverage and increase overall low-band capacity in those countries.”







In its reply, the Wider Spectrum Group suggests that this does not align with previous statements in the RSPG report on the UHF band published at the end of 2025. That report said: “The data clearly show that use of the 470–694 MHz band in the EU is currently dominated by DTTB [digital TV] and PMSE, and most of the regulators do not anticipate any significant change to this situation.”



We should not go into an accumulation of bands for IMT just for the sake of accumulation



The Wider Spectrum Group has numerous members, including the European Broadcasting Union (EBU), the European Federation of Journalists and the European Audiovisual Production Association. The group also said a previous RSPG statement – “A majority of [EU Member States] indicate a requirement to use the entire 470–694 MHz band for audio PMSE over the course of the next decade” – has not been fully taken into account.

“The Wider Spectrum Group is surprised that none of those facts, which reflect the views of countries, administrations and regulators, are mentioned in the draft 6G opinion,” it said. “This gives the impression that point 10 reflects only the viewpoint of some stakeholders, while prejudging the outcome of a complex process regarding specific discussions on the 470–694 MHz band and ignoring any impact on the incumbents of that essential band. This disrupts the balance of the draft 6G opinion in that respect.”

The group wants the final version of the Opinion to acknowledge the “limitations to be expected in the majority of EU Member States for 6G use of the 470–694 MHz band” outlined in the RSPG report on UHF. It also wants the RSPG to clarify that the 470–694 MHz band is “not a suitable option for 6G in Europe, at least for the foreseeable future,” because 6G “cannot be implemented in a harmonised way in the majority of EU Member States”.

PMSE concerns

The Wireless Microphone Spectrum Alliance (WMSA), an association that represents radio frequency coordinators and users of wireless microphones, said it “registers serious concern about paragraph 10 of the Draft Opinion, which identifies the 470-694 MHz band as a potential resource for ‘nationwide and indoor 6G coverage’ and announces that the RSPG and CEPT will ‘assess different scenarios for flexible use’ of this band for IMT”.

The 470-694 MHz band is, “from an operational standpoint, irreplaceable,” adds the statement. 

Guillaume Mascot of WMSA Europe told PolicyTracker that the 600 MHz band is “extensively used by PMSE… We should not go into an accumulation of bands for IMT just for the sake of accumulation”. Most of the time, 30-50% of PMSE equipment is running in the 600 MHz band, he added. 

National regulators should compile an impact assessment on the consequences of changes in the UHF band for programming and creativity in their respective countries. A flexible or local approach could be potentially considered so that PMSE can still use the spectrum. 

“We are really at the tipping point on our usage of UHF,” Mascot said. “Our end users are already doing a lot of magic on spectrum optimisation, and any reduction of spectrum will really have an impact on shows and other activities.”

WMSA’s response to the RPG’s draft Opinion highlights that the spectrum fragmentation would “devastate cross-border touring”. 

While the reduction from 700 and 800 MHz was manageable, “this one will not be,” added Mascot. The WMSA said paragraph 10 should be changed to take PMSE into account. 

“The RSPG should correct the omission of PMSE from paragraph 10, require full PMSE impact assessments in any scenario work, and commit to a minimum harmonised PMSE spectrum guarantee and to meaningful engagement with the RF coordination community throughout any scenario study process,” the organisatioin said•

Interference in a slice of the 800 MHz band used by IoT devices that generated over 120 complaints was caused by an automated lawnmower, says the French spectrum agency ANFR.



The agency first received an interference complaint from IoT (Internet of Things) device manufacturer UnaBiz in the unlicensed 868.034-868.226 MHz band. The spectrum supports a long-range (LoRa) network that is generally used to transmit small amounts of data from devices such as sensors, smart meters and services related to utilities and other industries.



An RTK base station was found to be the problem



While the band is not licensed in the same way as IMT spectrum, operators are still subject to strict technical conditions to allow coexistence between LoRa users in the band and to prevent interference.

The lawnmower causing interference carried a CE mark, indicating compliance with European Union regulations. However, it still breached the technical limits for the 863–870 MHz LoRa band, which is harmonised at the CEPT level, raising concerns that similar interference incidents could easily occur in other European markets using the same unlicensed spectrum.

The investigation

UnaBiz reported jamming affecting four of its radio sites in the French municipalities of Sainte-Colombe, Serpaize, Luzinay, and Valencin in spring 2024. The ANFR opened an investigation and dispatched teams to the regions south of Lyon to conduct field measurements to locate the source of interference.

Pinpointing the source to a remote area 17 kilometres away from the UnaBiz antennae, Saint-Georges-d’Espéranche, they found “a robotic lawnmower, traversing the grassy areas as if nothing was amiss”.



Spectral measurements performed by ANFR from the RTK module of the lawnmower. Source: ANFR



“The team went into the field with their equipment, which is a laboratory truck with a goniometer,” says Catherine Gabay, deputy director of spectrum monitoring and enforcement at the ANFR. A goniometer is a direction-finding device that determines the origin of a radio signal.

“They did the measurements and drove around until they found the source, and then finished the search on foot with a receiver and antenna,” she adds. “They found this lawnmower in the middle of nowhere.”

How was the lawnmower causing interference?

The lawnmower behind the interference was a Mammotion LUBA 2 AWD 5000X, equipped with several modules using different frequency bands. The mower uses Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, GNSS (global navigation satellite systems, such as GPS and Galileo), as well as RTK (real-time kinematic) radio.

Mammotion is an automated lawnmower manufacturer headquartered in Shenzen, China. It is active in more than 30 markets, including the US, Canada, much of Europe, and Australia.

In the EU, their main retailers are Amazon in France, Germany, Italy and Spain.

The main source of interference was the use of RTK, which works by allowing the mower to receive GNSS signals alongside those from a nearby base station module that regularly broadcasts real-time corrections, enabling centimetre-level accuracy. RTK is a common positioning technology for autonomous devices.

In this case, the RTK base station broadcasting to the mower communicated in the 863-870 MHz band, the band used by the LoRa network.



Location of the LoRa radio sites versus the RTK base station. Source: ANFR



ANFR agents performed an on-site spectrum analysis of the RTK radio module and found that the base station did not comply with regulations.

The problem was discovered to be its bandwidth consumption; instead of broadcasting infrequently (1-10% of the time), the base station was broadcasting signals 40% of the time it was on.

How did ANFR tackle the issue?

“We asked the person using the lawnmower to cease using it; they did so, and we didn’t issue a report to the prosecutor for this case,” says Gabay. “We were very proportionate.”

Further issues were reported affecting all French regions; this case was the start, and ANFR groups from across the country became involved.

There were more than 120 complaints of interference affecting the LoRa network that were sent to and processed by the ANFR between 2024 and 2025. The French distributor of the lawnmower, which had been alerted alongside users, was told to stop.

Finding a solution

The ANFR held regular meetings with UnaBiz during this period to allow the company to develop better methods of detecting interference.

A software update was issued for the RTK module by the manufacturer to correct the high power consumption, which was then verified by the ANFR. It has since been automatically distributed to all models and included in all subsequent versions.

“It’s quite difficult for the user,” says Gabay. “It’s nearly impossible to know he’s causing interference, especially as he had bought something that was supposed to comply with regulations.”

The proposed €20.35 billion deal would redraw France’s spectrum map by redistributing SFR’s spectrum holdings to Bouygues, Free Mobile and Orange.



Negotiations opened on 17 April between SFR (Altice France) and a consortium of Bouygues Telecom, Free–iliad Group and Orange. The transaction will lead to a spectrum redistribution exercise on a scale France has not seen since the market assumed its current four-operator structure in 2012.



The French competition authority has indicated it does not expect to clear the deal before 2027



The joint statement issued by the three buyers is explicit about how the assets would be carved up. Bouygues Telecom would absorb SFR’s B2B business and its mobile network in less-densely populated areas outright, while the B2C customer base, the rest of the infrastructure and SFR’s spectrum holdings would be shared between the three acquirers.

The split of price and value is 42% to Bouygues Telecom, 31% to Free–iliad and 27% to Orange. Unless the parties decide to split frequencies on a different basis from the rest of the deal, that ratio is the working assumption for how SFR’s mobile licences will be reassigned when the deal closes.

The implications for the underlying spectrum landscape are significant. SFR currently holds roughly a quarter of France’s commercially assigned sub-6 GHz mobile spectrum, with material positions in the 700 MHz, 800 MHz, 900 MHz, 1800 MHz, 2.1 GHz, 2.6 GHz and 3.4–3.8 GHz bands.



Bouygues Telecom is becoming the dominant spectrum holder. Orange, despite its 27% share of the price, ends up with the smallest incremental gain, consistent with the expectation that the former monopoly provider already holds the largest sub-1 GHz position.

Free, which entered the market in 2012 as France’s fourth player with the smallest spectrum endowment, closes the gap with Orange across the C-band and the 2.6 GHz range.



Current spectrum holdings by band. Source: PolicyTracker 2026



Bouygues Telecom’s chief financial officer, Christian Lecoq, told analysts on 7 May that the group “will keep 100% of the network we are sharing with SFR” under the existing Crozon network-sharing agreement, while dismantling SFR’s overlay in very densely populated areas.

For Free–iliad and Orange, the upside is different. Free is the consortium member with the most to gain in terms of acquiring spectrum under 1 GHz. Its rural reach has historically depended on a national roaming arrangement with Orange.

Orange says the operation is about investment capacity rather than spectrum. Speaking to the French Senate’s economic affairs committee on 6 May, chief executive Christel Heydemann said 19 of the 36 OECD member states operate with three national mobile network operators (MNOs) rather than four, and that seven of the ten best-performing European mobile markets in terms of internet speeds are three-player markets.

The three exceptions she cited – Denmark, Sweden and France – are four-operator markets where operators “practise extensive network mutualisation” in a declining market. Heydemann told senators that consolidation “will not push prices up” and argued that a broader customer base is the only sustainable way to reduce the fixed costs of mobile networks in a period of heavy investment in 5G and 6G.

Competition assessment

The fibre arm of SFR, XP Fibre, is excluded from the consortium’s perimeter and is on a separate sales track to infrastructure funds for a reported figure of around €8 billion, alongside other Altice France assets – ACS/Intelcia, Ultraedge and Altice Technical Services – plus the group’s overseas operations.

The competition assessment of the spectrum transfer will therefore proceed in parallel with, but not be bundled into, the assessment of France’s wholesale fibre market. The French competition authority has indicated it does not expect to clear the deal before 2027, and the European Commission is widely expected to take the first look, given the buyers’ combined revenue, before referring the French-market issues back to Paris.

The Commission’s recent review of its merger guidelines, prompted by the Draghi report on European competitiveness and now under a new competition directorate-general led by Anthony Whelan, will provide the policy backdrop.

The European regulators’ group BEREC has already warned that uncontrolled consolidation can produce tighter oligopolies and degrade competition in infrastructure. BEUC, the European Consumers’ Organisation, has cautioned against regulatory simplifications that weaken independent oversight.

Licence obligations

Spectrum caps do not formally apply in France, but the regulator Arcep can and does attach coverage, sharing and divestment obligations to its assignments. The 700 MHz, 800 MHz and 3.4–3.8 GHz licences awarded to SFR carry binding deployment commitments under the “New Deal Mobile”, agreed in 2017, and any transfer will require Arcep’s consent under the French electronic communications code (Article L. 42-3).

There are two licensing processes for the band: one for communities and another for regional internet service providers.



“There isn’t an auction for the communities’ licences,” says Sergio Sotomayor, director general of Colombian regulator ANE. Instead, the government will enable local communities to operate their own internet networks.



This model allows connectivity to be brought to areas where the traditional market has no incentive to operate



Licences will be for local 2 x 5 MHz blocks in the 897-902/942-947 MHz range.

According to the regulator, the terms “organised communities” or “connectivity communities” mentioned in the assignment conditions do not refer exclusively to regional authorities or territorial entities (such as cities or local governments).

They can refer to any group of people with a formal organisation, meaning they have legal status, share a territorial or community connection, and operate on a non-profit basis. They could include community action boards or other forms of community organisation.

The aim is for these communities to manage connectivity services for their own benefit, especially in areas where access has traditionally been limited.

“This model allows connectivity to be brought to areas where the traditional market has no incentive to operate, promoting community solutions that strengthen digital inclusion,” says the Colombian Ministry of Information and Communications Technologies (ICT).

The communities awarded licences will not have to pay for them, as this is part of the framework of the “public policy approach aimed at closing the digital divide,” the ministry says.



There will also be a separate process: an auction of spectrum for regional ISPs or mobile operators. The licences will be for local blocks of 2 x 10 MHz at 902-912/947-957 MHz.

The regulator says the ISP category is not limited to regional mobile operators and does not specifically exclude national operators. However, in practice, the process is more geared toward those seeking to offer internet services, primarily fixed-line.According to the regulator, this is because “traditional” mobile operators have already been awarded spectrum, and the characteristics of the 900 MHz band in this specific process make it less attractive for their current models. For this reason, the main interested parties will typically be small or medium-sized operators that provide internet services in specific cities or regions.

“If there is more than one ISP interested in some specific areas, the first step is to try to use technical conditions to make room for them both, for example, by specifying a certain distance between base stations,” says Sotomayor. “However, it is definitely not possible to have the operation of two or more ISPs in some areas, so we’ll have one bidding round based on economic offers.”      

Assignments are expected under both procedures in July. Interested parties have until this Wednesday (13 May 2026) to apply.

모바일 산업 협회는 유럽 규제 당국에 2025년부터 2035년 사이에 갱신 예정인 500개 이상의 주파수 라이센스에 대한 수수료를 줄이거나 없애줄 것을 요청하고 있습니다.



GSMA는 최근 보고서에서 “유럽에서 주파수 비용은 지난 10년 동안 거의 세 배로 증가했으며(수익의 비율로, 2014년 3%에서 2024년 8%로)”라고 언급했습니다.



GSMA의 주파수 정책 권고는 거의 전적으로 운영자에게 재정적으로 이익이 되는 레버에 집중하고 있습니다.



이들은 “이러한 변화는 높은 유보 가격, 연간 수수료 및 주파수 경매 설계와 같은 비시장적 요인에 의해 촉발되었으며, 이로 인해 사용 가능한 주파수의 양이 제한되고 주파수 비용이 인위적으로 증가했다”고 주장합니다. 운영자들이 2025년부터 2035년까지 현재 가격이 유지될 경우 누적 주파수 비용으로 1,040억 유로를 부담하게 된다는 수치가 제시되며, 이는 무료 갱신이 최대 300억 유로를 절약하고, 네트워크 속도를 최대 23% 향상시키며, 2035년까지 유럽 GDP에 최대 750억 유로를 추가할 수 있다는 주장을 뒷받침합니다. 보고서는 “자동 저비용 갱신이 시행된다면 향후 10년 동안 주파수 비용에서 최대 300억 유로를 절감할 수 있으며, 이는 추가 네트워크 투자 가능성을 개선할 것”이라고 설명합니다. 이 수치는 “유럽의 모든 기존 5G 네트워크를 5G SA로 업그레이드하는 데 필요한 투자보다도 초과하는 수치”입니다.



GSMA에 따르면, 유럽의 모바일 투자 필요성은 2026년 5월 기준입니다.



이 수치는 네 가지 시나리오 모델을 기반으로 합니다. 시나리오 1인 “매우 높은 비용”은 역사적 가격으로 갱신을 가정하며 1,040억 유로라는 수치를 도출합니다. 시나리오 4인 “저비용”은 무료 갱신을 가정하지만, 여전히 운영자들이 갱신 창구 이전에 취득한 기존 라이센스의 감가상각으로 590억 유로, 예상 연간 수수료로 170억 유로를 지불해야 한다고 가정합니다. 따라서 300억 유로의 절감은 두 가지 극단적인 시나리오 간의 차이일 뿐, 운영자의 지출이 절대적으로 줄어드는 것은 아닙니다. GDP 수치는 매개변수화된 단계들을 연결하여 구축됩니다. 주파수 비용 차이는 GSMA의 2025 글로벌 주파수 가격 책정 보고서에서 도출된 탄력성을 사용하여 네트워크 속도 개선으로 변환되며, 속도 개선은 보고서 부록에 명시된 가정에 따라 “브로드밴드 속도가 두 배로 증가하면 GDP 성장에 0.3% 기여할 수 있다”는 가정을 통해 GDP로 변환됩니다. 이 모델은 데이터 가용성 문제로 불가리아, 에스토니아, 라트비아, 룩셈부르크 및 스위스를 제외하며, 시나리오 2에서 4까지의 경우 연간 수수료가 감가상각 비용에 비례하여 감소한다고 가정하는데, 이는 정책적으로 중립적이지 않은 가정입니다.

의심스러운 가정

GSMA가 선택한 기준선은 국가들이 역사적 가격으로 라이센스를 갱신할 것이라고 가정합니다. 그러나 몇몇 유럽 규제 당국은 2010년대 2.1GHz 가격을 다시 부과할 의사를 표명한 바가 없으며, 스페인과 프랑스의 최근 갱신은 이미 연장 기반 또는 저비용 모델로 이동하고 있습니다.



GSMA의 자체 사례 연구도 이를 인정합니다: 스페인의 2024년 연장은 “구형 주파수 라이센스를 최근 스페인 라이센스 수여와 일치시켰으며,” 운영자들은 “최소 40년까지 라이센스를 유지”하게 되었다고 언급합니다. 또 다른 문제는 300억 유로의 가능한 절감입니다. 가장 관대한 정책 결과를 가장 가혹한 결과와 비교하면 실제 절감액이 부풀려지며, 이는 예측하기 훨씬 더 어려운 수치입니다.



시나리오 4에서의 23% 속도 향상은 또한 4%에서 시작하는 범위의 상한선입니다. 보고서 자체는 “무료 갱신(저비용 시나리오)으로 인해 네트워크 속도가 4%에서 23% 사이로 향상될 수 있다고 추정한다”고 명시하고 있으며, 이는 보고서의 주요 내용에는 반영되지 않은 범위입니다.



마찬가지로 유럽이 “더 이상 글로벌 모바일 연결성의 선두주자가 아니다”라는 진단은 원인과 결과가 여러 방향으로 흐르는 지표에 부분적으로 의존합니다: 분산된 국가 시장, 낮은 사용자당 평균 수익, 수요 측 약화 등이 GSMA의 자체 분석에 포함되어 있으며, 이는 주파수 정책이 여러 요인 중 하나임을 시사합니다. 그러나 이 보고서에서 GSMA의 주파수 정책 권고는 거의 전적으로 운영자에게 재정적으로 이익이 되는 레버에 집중하고 있습니다.

The mobile industry association wants European regulators to reduce or eliminate fees on the more than 500 spectrum licences due for renewal between 2025 and 2035.



The GSMA says in a recent report that “in Europe, the cost of spectrum has almost tripled over the past 10 years (as a proportion of revenue, from 3% in 2014 to 8% in 2024)”.



The GSMA’s policy recommendations on spectrum focus almost entirely on the lever that benefits operators financially



It claims that this “step change has been driven by non-market factors, such as high reserve prices, annual fees and auction designs that have limited the amount of spectrum available and therefore artificially increased spectrum costs”.

The headline number, that operators face €104 billion in cumulative spectrum costs over the 2025–2035 period if current pricing is maintained, is used to argue that free-of-charge renewals could save up to €30 billion, lift network speeds by as much as 23% and add up to €75 billion to European GDP by 2035.

“If automatic low-cost renewals are implemented, it could save up to €30 billion in spectrum costs over the next 10 years, improving the viability of further network investment,” the report says. This figure “exceeds the investment that is needed to upgrade to 5G SA across all existing 5G networks in Europe”.



Mobile investment needs in Europe according to the GSMA, May 2026



The numbers are based on a four-scenario model. Scenario 1, “very high cost,” assumes renewals at historical prices and produces the €104 billion figure. Scenario 4, “low cost,” assumes free-of-charge renewals, but still leaves operators paying €59 billion in amortisation of existing licences acquired before the renewal window, plus €17 billion in projected annual fees. The €30 billion saving is therefore the gap between the two most extreme scenarios, not an absolute reduction in operator outgoings.

The GDP figure is built by chaining parameterised steps. Spectrum cost differences are converted into network speed improvements using elasticities drawn from the GSMA’s 2025 Global Spectrum Pricing paper, and speed improvements are then converted into GDP using the assumption, set out in the report’s appendix, that “a doubling of broadband speeds can contribute 0.3% to GDP growth”.

The model excludes Bulgaria, Estonia, Latvia, Luxembourg and Switzerland on data-availability grounds, and assumes annual fees fall in proportion to amortised costs in scenarios two through four, an assumption that is not policy-neutral.

The US regulator has accepted proposals that will see a new system introduced in the country to replace the ITU’s current interference protections for satellites in geostationary orbit (GSO).



The FCC accepted the proposals outlined in its “Modernizing Spectrum Sharing” report and order on 30 April, after considering complaints from several GSO satellite operators, including DirecTV and SES.



Those rules were designed for a different generation of satellite systems



The move comes as the regulator recognises the increasing investment into non-geostationary (NGSO) satellites in low-Earth orbit (LEO) from companies like Starlink and Amazon Leo.

Currently, around 10,000 Starlink NGSO satellites are in LEO, where they provide terrestrial broadband from space.

PolicyTracker understands that the FCC framework still requires compliance with ITU rules on EPFD for international operations, but allows exceedance within US territory where permitted under ITU rules.

EPFD limits

The FCC believes that EPFD (equivalent power flux density) limits – a measure introduced by the ITU in the 1990s to limit interference from NGSO satellites in the Ka (26.5-40 GHz) and Ku (12-18 GHz) bands – are outdated and do not account for recent developments in the satellite world.

EPFD was a topic discussed at WRC-23. Amazon and SpaceX pushed for a future agenda item to update the rules, which was countered by GSO operators.

EPFD limits are not on the formal agenda for WRC-27, though there will be studies as part of the preparations for the event.

New developments

New technologies such as adaptive coding and modulation (ACM) allow satellites to adjust their signals dynamically to suit conditions, the FCC argues, enabling them to maintain connection even when there is interference.

Additionally, the FCC believes EPFD limits restrict the number of LEO satellites that can operate in any given area at the same time and so prevent rural areas of the US from getting access to the fastest internet speeds, as current spectrum is not “being exploited enough”.

“For years, our framework has relied on legacy constructs like EPFD,” said FCC chair Brendan Carr. “Those rules were designed for a different generation of satellite systems, one defined by a small number of geostationary operators and primitive networks. That is not the world we live in any more.”

Carr adds that “modern satellite designs make it far easier to share spectrum,” and regulations drafted more than 26 years ago cannot take this into account.

Current regulations and changes

Hard limits currently exist on EPFD in the 10.7-30 GHz shared satellite spectrum range, which is the range relied on for satellite broadband from NGSO satellites. EPFD downlink limits apply in the 10.7-12.7, 17.3-18.6, and 19.7-20.2 GHz bands in the US, and all NGSO applicants must comply with those limits.



The proposal ‘moves beyond rigid EPFD limits in the Ku- and Ka- downlink bands and towards a framework grounded in good-faith coordination’



The FCC is removing them. The EPFD framework is to be replaced with new GSO protection criteria, and the FCC will now try to encourage “good faith co-ordination” between operators. These private agreements will be supported by several technical backstops:



A long-term protection criterion of 3% signal degradation for GSO links using ACM

A protection criterion of -10.5 dB interference-to-noise for 80% of the time to support GSO links that do not use ACM

A supplemental protection requirement for NGSO systems to observe a minimum 3-degree avoidance angle of the GSO arc, a reduction from the current regime’s typical 12–15-degree avoidance arc



All existing protection requirements for sharing and protecting radio astronomy services are to be untouched and are underpinned by “private negotiations where the current EPFD regime has prevented beneficial bargaining”.

A practical outcome of this, according to the FCC’s research, is that NGSO operators would be able to increase the number of satellites operating simultaneously in a given area from one to “as many as eight”.

The change in avoidance arc “would increase the number of satellites available to serve an Earth station location” and increase coverage.

FCC commissioner Olivia Trusty says the proposal “promotes more effective spectrum sharing by moving beyond rigid EPFD limits in the Ku- and Ka- downlink bands and toward a framework grounded in good-faith coordination”.

Criticism from incumbents

Several GSO operators have written to the FCC to recommend changes or raise concerns. One significant critic is DirecTV, which operates several GSO satellites for broadcasting.

Concerns about interference prompted the company to write to the FCC, urging that the GSO avoidance angle be increased to 4.5 degrees as the “benefits of increasing the arc… are substantial for the protection of GSO networks”.

The company also backed several proposals by Luxembourg-based satellite operator SES, calling for the adoption of a further notice of proposed rulemaking on the “development of aggregate limits on NGSO interference into GSO networks” alongside more clarification on several technical parameters.

Both Amazon and Starlink wrote to the FCC to reject these concerns, Amazon claiming that DirecTV’s request to expand avoidance angles “relies exclusively on its analysis of a single test report”.

Amazon also claims the company is one “of a parade of GSO operators, that, after declining to propose reference links for 355 days after the Commission requested input on representative GSO reference links, decided that the several days leading up to the notice… is absolutely the ideal time”.

Final decision of the FCC

Despite these concerns, the FCC has adopted the measures and is now set to abolish EPFD limits and move to its new good-faith system.

“US companies are leading the world in next-generation satellite services, said Carr. “We should have a regulatory framework that matches that energy, not one that slows it down.”•

Australia has moved from heavy constraints to voluntary measures to manage coexistence between mobile services in the 3.7-4.0 GHz band and radio altimeters.





ACMA ‘strongly encourages licensees to follow these measures’



The Australian Communications and Media Authority (ACMA) says that now that the Australian aviation regulator CASA has mandated new radio altimeters (RAs) that comply with the US FAA Airworthiness Directive (FAA AD), “the risk of interference can be managed without the interim mitigations in place”.

The new framework takes effect from this month until June 2029, with a review in Q1 2028 to take stock of global work on a new Minimum Operational Performance Standard (MOPS) for next‑generation radio altimeters.

ACMA has presented the new coexistence framework as voluntary because it sits in an advisory note rather than in binding licence conditions. However, in practice, it exerts strong pressure on operators to adapt their networks. The regulator “strongly encourages licensees to follow these measures,” warning that “any evidence of interference from IMT base stations into radio altimeters may necessitate consideration of more robust measures”.

The authority is also declining to exempt existing base stations and instructing assignment staff not to register non‑exempt transmitters in restriction zones that exceed the new limits.

From exclusion zones to voluntary measures

In 2023, ACMA introduced interim mitigations that barred new IMT deployments above 3.7 GHz in exclusion zones around selected airports and imposed restricted‑zone power levels further out, initially until March 2025 and eventually until March 2026. Those measures were framed as a temporary response until the aviation sector could retrofit better‑performing altimeters.

The outcomes paper published by ACMA confirmed that the interim regime would “end on 31 March 2026 as planned”. Since April 2026, ACMA has been relying on tighter unwanted‑emission limits in the radio‑altimeter band, applied inside defined “restriction zones” around a subset of runways.

ACMA stresses that the design is anchored in the US Federal Aviation Authority’s (FAA) interference tolerance mask for “radio‑altimeter‑tolerant” aircraft. CASA has now mandated that, for specified low‑visibility instrument procedures, “aircraft Radio Altimeters must meet the FAA AD tolerance levels,” with landing restrictions applied to aircraft that have not yet been retrofitted.

Inner and outer zones: easing constraints away from the runway



We are not aware of any confirmed cases of interference to radio altimeters that have been attributed to (WBB) mobile transmitters in Australia



One of the mobile industry’s key demands was for a more granular geography so that constraints are toughest close to the runway and relaxed further out. ACMA accepts this logic and will split each restriction zone into an inner zone and an outer zone, both derived from its earlier propagation analysis but assuming different worst‑case spurious emission levels.

A restriction zone is defined as two parts:



A “first zone” comprising three runway‑length segments, including 1,380 metres from the landing end, 250 metres at the opposite end and the runway itself, each 250 metres either side of the centreline.

A “second zone” that extends 6,820 metres beyond each end of the first zone.



Within these zones, ACMA sets explicit spurious‑emission expectations in the 4.2–4.4 GHz altimeter band for both MIMO and non‑MIMO base stations, framed as registration recommendations for the transmitters.

Voluntary approach

The aviation industry had argued for mandatory conditions that would remain in force until new international altimeter standards are implemented and compliant equipment is widely available. ACMA rejected that, emphasising the lack of demonstrated interference cases in Australia and the degree of headroom already available in commercial 5G equipment.

“To date, we are not aware of any confirmed cases of interference to radio altimeters that have been attributed to (WBB) mobile transmitters in Australia,” the paper notes. It adds that “WBB (mobile) transmitters are highly likely to already comply with the reduced spurious emissions limits specified in the voluntary approach” and that the regime has been developed “in consultation with, and agreed to by, CASA”.

ACMA concludes that “a voluntary approach is appropriate in these circumstances and is consistent with the current approach implemented in the US, where [mobile network operators] have agreed to adhere to stricter spurious emission limits (that is, these emission limits have not been mandated in the US)”.

On the mobile industry side, operators sought to keep existing base stations within the zones under the old rules, arguing that verifying compliance with tighter radio altimeter band limits could be costly and disruptive, especially given that these sites have not been reported to generate interference.

ACMA again declined: “We are of the view that existing base stations should not be grandfathered,” it says. “The assurance measures are a response to the mandate of a new RA requirement, which will have different performance to those fitted on existing aircraft.”

Licensees have to notify ACMA of their intention to register a mobile base station near airports, four weeks before registration. The aim is to give the aviation sector “advanced awareness” so that flight scheduling can ensure FAA‑AD‑compliant altimeters are available where necessary.

Global picture

Globally, regulators still treat 5G and radio‑altimeter coexistence as a live aviation‑safety issue, but responses vary. The US is moving towards mandatory, interference‑tolerant altimeters and large‑scale retrofits, while Europe and many other regions largely rely on technical mitigations such as guard bands, power limits and protection zones around airports.

The latest guidance from the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) notes that many states are using temporary restrictions and coordination procedures while they gather more data and wait for upgraded altimeter standards to be fully implemented. The ICAO suggests that states should plan for retrofit programmes of up to about 10 years after the new performance standards and technical standard orders are available, so many fleets are likely to be transitioning well into the 2030s rather than by 2029•

How does NextNav’s network operate?

NextNav’s product is distinct from the most widely used PNT service in the US, GPS, and is described as a 3D geolocation service that would improve positioning indoors.

GPS consists of 24-31 satellites in medium-Earth orbit (MEO), which broadcast L-band (1-2 GHz) signals to terrestrial terminals and receivers. It then uses the time it takes for the signal to reach the receiver to calculate location – a process called trilateration.

NextNav’s network is entirely terrestrial and relies on the lower 900 MHz band, as it is based on 5G and uses 5G base stations.



A diagram explaining NextNav’s network. Source: NextNav



NextNav’s network relies on 5G positioning reference signal (PRS), a method in which base stations broadcast standardised 5G signals that a user’s device receives from multiple base stations simultaneously.

The receiving device then calculates the arrival time of these signals and, by comparing the time difference between multiple base stations, determines the 3D location of the user.

The argument in favour of terrestrial PNT is that it provides resilience for the US government and economy should GPS be targeted by jamming and spoofing or go down completely.

Regulators are critical

Europe’s regulators have already been critical of the European Commission’s proposed DNA, warning that some of its ideas on spectrum and other “common Union resources” risk over‑centralisation, weaker contestability and greater legal uncertainty for national regulatory authorities (NRAs) and mobile network operators.

The European regulators’ group BEREC stated in a preliminary reaction that the Commission’s approach “marks a substantial shift to the current approach” by treating spectrum as a “common European resource under shared competence”, introducing spectrum rights of indefinite duration, establishing “a common approach for spectrum fees” and creating “a single EU‑level satellite authorisation framework”.

BEREC is particularly uneasy about the DNA’s potential impact on market structure and spectrum access, saying the proposal “seems to favour the position of present rights holders, thereby weakening market contestability and hindering the currently functioning competitive dynamics in access to spectrum resources, to the detriment of innovation, efficient spectrum use and long‑term consumer benefits”.

According to BEREC, the planned single market procedure, which includes a Commission veto on auction proposals, “does not seem justified and proportionate”.

BEREC links this to a wider trend of shifting powers “from NRAs to the European Commission and from BEREC to the ODN”. It asks for evidence that the approach proposed by the Commission “would effectively contribute to a more functional internal market and to simplification, predictability and effective regulation”.

The ODN (Office for Digital Networks) is a new body proposed in the DNA that would support and assist BEREC and a new Radio Spectrum Policy Board (RSPB), which would replace and take over the tasks of the Radio Spectrum Policy Group (with a remit for spectrum matters).

“The necessary regulatory and supervisory powers of NRAs are best placed to address any specific national market conditions,” states BEREC.

According to Sweden’s Post and Telecom Authority (PTS), the DNA would fundamentally weaken national control over spectrum regulation and centralise too much power in Brussels. PTS says European harmonisation is very valuable but stresses that it must still allow national and regional specificities to be taken into account.

As the DNA is a regulation (rather than a directive), PTS argues that spectrum use within its scope would be governed almost entirely by EU rules, leaving regulators to resolve difficult borderline cases such as aviation and maritime systems with little national discretion.

The authority predicts “significant legal uncertainty in the member states’ work on radio spectrum management and licensing” and even questions whether Sweden’s roughly 10,000 annual licensing cases can “be carried out in an appropriate manner at all” in the first years after implementation.

It also criticises the move to unlimited mobile licence terms and an implicit presumption of renewal, warning that this would “severely hamper, or completely prevent, the entry of new mobile operators” and amount to “a transfer of wealth from the state to the licence holder”. Overall, PTS calls for a “fundamental re‑draft” of the spectrum chapter and insists that security‑related licence conditions must remain possible at the national level.

Some proposals in the act have been received more positively, BEREC notes, such as “the intention to provide more clarity on its role in the market shaping aspects of spectrum assignment, which may constructively support competent authorities in their spectrum assignment duties, where necessary”.

It also backs “continuing to develop roadmaps on radio spectrum together, where appropriate, with national radio spectrum roadmaps” to “strengthen a shared EU-wide vision of upcoming spectrum resources”.

BEREC sees the proposal for a centrally managed satellite regime as having “merit in principle”.
